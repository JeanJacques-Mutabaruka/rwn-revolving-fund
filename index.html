Strategic Objective

What the Revolving Fund is designed to achieve

The revolving fund is RWN's answer to a fundamental question: how do you build lasting economic independence for frontline women leaders — not dependency on periodic grants, but self-sustaining financial capacity rooted in their own businesses and communities?

Objective 01
💰
Provide affordable capital
Give Women Safe Space Facilitators (WSSFs) access to working capital at 2%/month — far below commercial MFI rates (5–15%) and informal lenders (10–30%) — to finance income-generating activities (IGAs) they could not otherwise launch or scale.
Objective 02
📈
Build profitable, autonomous businesses
The fund does not merely lend money — it accompanies each WSSF through the full business cycle: from business plan development through market linkage to repayment and growth. Repayments return to the fund, enabling more women to be served each cycle.
Objective 03
🌍
Strengthen community leadership
Economically empowered WSSFs become more effective community leaders — better able to sustain Safe Spaces, mentor other women, and drive governance, GBV prevention, and environmental resilience in their districts. Economic autonomy amplifies social impact.
The Case for Change

From grants that subsidise to capital that empowers

The global development landscape is undergoing a structural shift. The traditional model — where NGOs distribute grants to subsidise activities and beneficiaries remain permanently dependent on donor cycles — is being challenged by evidence, by financing constraints, and by a new generation of women leaders who want tools, not handouts. Rwanda Women's Network is positioned to lead this transition.

🇷🇼 Rwandan context
  • →Rwanda's Vision 2050 and NST1 explicitly target financial inclusion and women's economic empowerment as national priorities — the revolving fund is fully aligned with government strategy.
  • →Despite Rwanda's strong gender equality legal framework, women in rural and peri-urban areas face persistent barriers: limited collateral, distance from formal banks, low literacy, and seasonal income volatility.
  • →Informal savings groups (VSLAs, tontines) are widespread but insufficient: they provide small, short-term loans at high rates (10–30%/month) with no business accompaniment — trapping entrepreneurs in subsistence cycles.
  • →Post-harvest losses, fertiliser price volatility, and market access gaps systematically undermine agricultural IGAs — the most common livelihood for RWN's target population — without structural market linkage support.
🌍 International context
  • →ODA funding is contracting globally. USAID, the EU, and bilateral donors are shifting from grant-based programming toward blended finance, reimbursable instruments, and sustainability-linked disbursements — making self-sustaining models like revolving funds strategically essential.
  • →The 2030 SDG agenda (SDG 1, 5, 8, 10) explicitly calls for sustainable financial inclusion models that outlast project cycles. Revolving funds are among the most evidence-backed mechanisms for durable grassroots impact.
  • →Multi-variable volatility — simultaneous shocks in fertiliser prices (+80% post-2022), supply chains (geopolitical disruptions), and climate (irregular rainfall) — disproportionately hits the most vulnerable women entrepreneurs, creating a critical need for resilience-based accompaniment, not just capital.
  • →Impact investors and green finance instruments increasingly seek investable vehicles at the grassroots level. A well-structured revolving fund with an SPV can attract institutional capital that grant-based programmes never could.

RWN's Innovation

What makes the RWN model distinctive

RWN is not building a classic microfinance institution, nor replicating a grant programme. The model is a third way — combining the discipline of a lending institution with the proximity and accompaniment of a community organisation, and adding the market connectivity of a business ecosystem. None of these three elements alone is sufficient. Together, they create something genuinely new in the Rwandan NGO landscape.

🏦
1. Disciplined lending
Structured loan cycle, clear eligibility criteria, repayment discipline, and portfolio monitoring — with the governance and transparency needed to attract institutional investors and maintain donor confidence. The fund is designed to be financially sustainable, not perpetually subsidised.
🤝
2. Customised accompaniment
Monthly field staff visits are not just repayment check-ins — they are business health assessments and early warning systems. AI-guided business plan building in Kinyarwanda removes the eligibility barrier for less-educated women. Peer learning within Safe Space networks multiplies the coaching effect without proportionally increasing costs.
🌐
3. Four-sided marketplace
Market linkage is the missing link in most microfinance programmes. RWN connects WSSFs to input suppliers (buy-side), institutional buyers (sell-side), processing companies that multiply their product value (strategic partners), and impact investors (finance side) — turning isolated borrowers into integrated value-chain actors.
🔄 Capital recycling and progressive growth
Unlike grants, every repaid loan goes back into the fund and finances the next borrower. Each successful cycle grows the fund's capital base and track record, enabling RWN to attract additional investment on progressively better terms. A WSSF who repays her first loan qualifies for a larger second loan — creating a visible, measurable graduation pathway from subsistence to financial autonomy. Over the 2025–2030 cycle, the fund is designed to grow from RWF 26M to RWF 150M in microloan disbursements.
⚡ Resilience by design — not afterthought
In an era of simultaneous multi-variable shocks — fertiliser prices, climate events, geopolitical disruptions, ODA freezes — most microfinance models have no answer for borrowers facing crises through no fault of their own. RWN's model builds resilience in: grace periods, buy-side market access to alternative suppliers, processing partnerships that reduce post-harvest losses, and early warning protocols triggered by field staff data. This converts loan management into active business resilience management.
Strategic alignment

Grounded in RWN's 2020–2030 Strategic Plan

The revolving fund is not a standalone initiative — it is the financial expression of RWN's three-pillar theory of change and directly serves Strategic Objective SO3 (Output 2.2).

Pillar 01
💚
Heal
Safe Spaces are the trust infrastructure on which the fund is built. WSSFs are the very facilitators of healing — economic stability reinforces their ability to create and sustain safe environments for other women.
Pillar 02
💪
Empower
The revolving fund is RWN's core economic empowerment vehicle: affordable capital + business accompaniment + market linkage, delivered through a self-sustaining mechanism that grows stronger with each cycle.
Pillar 03
🌱
Transform
Financially autonomous WSSFs become more effective community leaders — sustaining Safe Spaces beyond project cycles, mentoring peers, and driving governance and resilience in their districts. Economic independence amplifies social transformation.
